Chronergy126
Race Simulation Platform

Project Overview
Chronergy126 is a cloud-based web application built to centralise race engineering workflows: session planning, telemetry analysis, simulation, strategy, and reporting, all in one platform. The project grew out of a concrete gap identified on the pit wall — existing tools were fragmented, slow, and not built for the pace of a race weekend. I built this to close that gap.

My Role
I led the project end-to-end as product owner and lead front-end developer. My work spanned the full product lifecycle: concept definition, UX architecture in Figma, developer handoff, user story review, acceptance testing, and ultimately direct front-end development using the Figma-to-code pipeline in Cursor with Claude Code.
Managing a 6-person remote team in Romania while maintaining quality and velocity required building a clear process from scratch — structured reviews, defined acceptance criteria, and a CLAUDE.md context file to keep AI-assisted development consistent across sessions.
Core Engineering Challenge
The hardest technical problem was scaling simulation throughput per user. A single simulation runs in ~30 seconds on one machine. The solution: parallel cloud execution across multiple Azure instances, dropping 200 simulations from days of sequential compute to approximately 25 minutes wall-clock time.
This required rethinking the job queuing architecture and how the backend distributed workloads — a problem with real consequences for whether the platform is usable during a live race weekend or just in testing environments.
Why It Exists
Race engineering tooling is stuck in a world of spreadsheets, disconnected apps, and information scattered across a dozen windows. Chronergy126 brings all of it into one place: session plan, tyre allocation tracker, lap time analysis, setup history, simulation runs, and stint reports. The underlying simulation physics — full multibody transient dynamics, a thermal tyre model reverse-engineered from on-track data, virtual 7-post rig analysis, and auto track bump profile reconstruction from logged car data — were developed through years of applied work at the intersection of on-track performance and software.

Stint Report Pipeline

At Octane126, post-stint analysis was done entirely by hand. The data engineer would download car data from the logger, open the analysis software, navigate through channels tab by tab, manually identify anomalies using KPIs they held in their heads, then build a report by printing individual graphs one at a time. Two hours per stint, with no guarantee every relevant graph made it in.

SOCOFER

Mechanical Design Intern
Railway Engineering
SolidWorks Thermal Runaway Li-Ion Battery Railway

Assigned to the TFE (Traction, Braking, Energy) department at a globally recognised railway vehicle manufacturer. The brief: design a lithium-ion battery housing capable of actively suppressing thermal runaway while keeping the pack sealed and the solution compliant with railway safety standards — a problem with no clean off-the-shelf answer.

The challenge with thermal runaway in Li-ion cells is that you need to attack the cells directly, not just contain the exterior. The solution I developed integrates a trap-door mechanism — triggered on thermal runaway detection — that connects to a glycol water circuit ("colonne humide") stored in onboard tanks. This allows suppression fluid to reach the cells internally while the housing stays sealed under normal conditions.

The system also provides a standardised dry column connection point ("colonne sèche") for fire services to plug in directly — a detail that bridges the onboard system with emergency response infrastructure. Full CAD model delivered in SolidWorks.
